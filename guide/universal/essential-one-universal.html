Essential one / Stillness

The ability
to sit in
silence.

A little less input.
A little more room to think.

You are allowed
to pause.

For young people aged 13–23.
Adapted from Brendan O’Keefe’s onetoeight guide.

Essential one01 / The familiar bit

You only came
to check the time.

An imagined scene. Possibly a familiar one.

It’s 11.47pm. You pick up your phone to set an alarm.

Somehow, you’re now watching a man build a swimming pool in a forest. You have neither a forest nor plans for a swimming pool. Still, you feel you should see how it turns out.

Then there’s a message. A video. A comment so wrong it apparently requires your personal attention.

You put the phone down. In the quiet, tomorrow turns up: the unfinished work, the conversation you’re avoiding, the feeling you haven’t quite named.

You pick the phone up again.

You might recognise that loop. Or your version might involve gaming, podcasts, messages or keeping the television on when you’re not watching it. There’s always something available to fill a gap.

Sometimes that’s useful. Music can make a difficult day bearable. A message can connect you with someone who understands. A ridiculous video can be exactly what you need. Enjoying those things isn’t the problem.

The question is whether you still get a choice. Can you leave a small space unfilled, or does your hand reach for something before you’ve decided?

This essential is about practising that choice. You’re making room to notice what’s happening around you and inside you, before the next thing asks for your attention.

You don’t have to fill
every available moment.

Here, silence means a short break from deliberately adding more input. You can hear traffic, move about or sit near other people. You don’t need an empty house or a mind with nothing in it. You certainly don’t need to become a person who says “my morning ritual” without laughing.

Start with a minute. You can decide what happens after that.

Essential one02 / Where your attention goes

Your attention
has plenty of offers.

An endless feed has no final page. Autoplay makes the next decision for you. A notification interrupts whatever you were doing, even when it’s announcing something as urgent as a discount on socks.

These features make continuing easy and stopping something you have to choose. That matters when you’re tired, bored or worried. It helps explain why “I’ll just have more willpower” can be a frustrating plan.

There’s no need to dress this up as a dramatic story about your brain being broken. Dopamine is involved in motivation and learning, among other functions. It isn’t a fuel tank that empties when you put down your phone. Feeling restless in the quiet doesn’t, by itself, mean you’re addicted.

Look at what a habit is doing for you

The same action can serve different needs. You might check a chat because you’re lonely, open a video because you need a laugh, or scroll because starting an assignment feels horrible. Sometimes you’re simply enjoying yourself.

Before changing a habit, get curious about it. “What was I looking for just then?” is more useful than “Why am I like this?”

If you need company, a proper conversation might help more than watching people you don’t know. If you need rest, another half-hour of scrolling may leave you just as tired. If you need help with work, asking someone might get you further than avoiding it.

A small investigation

The next time you reach for your phone without a clear reason, pause. Name what you were doing, what you were feeling and what you hoped the phone would provide. Then choose whether to use it.

You’re allowed to choose yes. The pause still counts.

Connection counts, wherever it happens

A friend on the other side of the country is still a friend. Online communities can offer support you don’t have nearby. This chapter isn’t asking you to give that up.

It asks you to notice the difference between a conversation you value and a feed you keep refreshing despite feeling worse. The useful boundary may be around a particular app, a time of day or a kind of content, rather than every screen in your life.

Your phone does several jobs. You don’t have to sack the entire workforce because one department keeps interrupting.

Essential one03 / Try it before reading more

Your first
quiet minute.

Pick a moment when you’re somewhere safe and don’t need to watch traffic, supervise someone or respond to anything urgent.

  1. Make a little space.

    Sit comfortably, stand or move gently in a safe space. Keep your eyes open if you prefer. There is no official sitting position. Your knees have not signed up for anything.

  2. Reduce the interruptions.

    Put your phone out of easy reach, if practical. Keep any medical, accessibility, safety or caring functions you need. A timer is optional. If you use your phone, set a gentle timer and leave the screen face down.

  3. Choose something to notice.

    Try the contact between your body and the chair, an object in front of you, or a sound nearby. You can notice your breathing if that feels comfortable, but don’t force it to change.

  4. Notice when your mind wanders.

    You’ll remember a message, wonder about lunch or start planning next Tuesday. When you notice, gently return your attention to what you chose. You haven’t ruined the minute. Returning is part of the practice.

  5. Finish without giving yourself a grade.

    After roughly a minute, look around. Ask, “What did I notice?” One ordinary observation is enough: “I’m tired”, “I kept wanting to check”, or “The fridge is surprisingly loud.”

Keep the useful part

If sitting still is uncomfortable, try slow movement. If silence makes tinnitus or sensory discomfort worse, try gentle background sound. If focusing inside your body is unpleasant, look at something outside it. You can adapt the exercise or leave it.

There’s no need to feel calmer afterwards. You’ve tried a short pause and collected some information. If it was manageable, you can try again another day. If it wasn’t, the next section offers a different way in.

Essential one04 / What you might notice

A wandering mind
is still a mind.

The first quiet minute might feel restful, boring, irritating or no different from any other minute. You might spend most of it wondering whether it’s finished. There isn’t a fixed sequence you’re supposed to move through.

You don’t have to empty your mind. Thoughts will arrive. Your job is to notice them without treating every one as an instruction.

“I need to reply right now” might be a genuine responsibility. It might also be a habit, or a worry about disappointing someone. A pause gives you a chance to check which.

Noticing isn’t the same as solving

Suppose you realise you’re anxious about tomorrow. You don’t have to fix tomorrow during this minute. Afterwards, you might write down one next step: pack what you need, ask a question or tell someone you’re worried.

Quiet can help you notice a feeling. It doesn’t make every thought accurate. “Everyone thinks I’m an idiot” is a painful thought, not a completed opinion poll. You can question it, talk it through or let it pass without arguing with it for the rest of the evening.

When quiet feels worse

Some people find that turning down outside noise makes difficult thoughts or memories feel louder. If you feel panicky, detached from your surroundings or overwhelmed, stop the exercise. Look around, notice a few familiar objects, move if that helps and contact someone you trust.

You don’t need to prove anything by staying in distress. Try a shorter pause, gentle movement, background sound or being near a supportive person. If it keeps happening, speak with a trusted adult, counsellor, GP or another qualified professional.

A practice, not a treatment plan

This chapter offers an everyday attention exercise. It doesn’t diagnose anything or replace mental healthcare. Research on meditation and mindfulness suggests benefits for some people, but findings vary and some people report adverse effects. That research doesn’t prove that this particular one-minute exercise will help everyone.

A useful outcome can be “I’d rather try this while walking” or “I need support with what came up”. Learning what works for you is more important than reaching a longer time.

Essential one05 / Make it easier to stop

Change the set-up.
Spare the lecture.

It’s difficult to practise quiet while a device keeps inviting you elsewhere. Make one small change that helps you pause. You don’t need to redesign your entire life before Thursday.

Decide what deserves to interrupt you

Look at your notifications. Keep the ones that matter for safety, health, access, caring responsibilities and genuinely important contact. Consider switching off promotional alerts, suggested posts and updates you can check later.

A human message isn’t automatically urgent, either. You can care about your friends without responding to every group-chat observation in real time. Let people know when you’re usually available and how to reach you if something matters.

Give your hand an extra decision

Move the app you open automatically off your home screen. Turn off autoplay where you can. Put your phone in a bag rather than beside your hand during one activity. The point is to interrupt a habit long enough to choose, not to make your phone unusable.

If greyscale makes your screen less appealing, you can try it. If it makes things harder to see or use, skip it. A setting is useful only if it helps you.

Attach a pause to something that already happens

After brushing your teeth. Before opening your laptop. Once you’ve come home and put your bag down. Choose a moment that exists in your actual day, rather than the imaginary day in which you wake up early and become a completely different person.

Write a specific plan: “After I put my bag down, I’ll sit by the window for one minute before opening a feed.” If that moment is chaotic, choose another.

Work with the home you have

A busy house, shared bedroom or unpredictable routine may make private quiet difficult. You could try a library, a supervised quiet area at school, or a familiar safe place outdoors. You can also practise with ordinary household sound around you.

If you need your phone nearby for care, access or safety, keep it nearby. If you’re walking, carry what you need, use a safe route and stay aware of your surroundings. Being less distracted should never mean being less safe.

Choose just one change

Which interruption could you reduce without losing something important? Try that change for a few days. Keep it if it helps; adjust it if it doesn’t.

Essential one06 / A week to experiment

Seven days.
No perfect streak.

Use this as an experiment, not a test of character. Start with a minute or less. Stay at that length all week if it suits you. If it feels comfortable, try two minutes, then perhaps five. Longer isn’t automatically better.

01

Try the quiet minute. Notice what happens. Change your position, focus or surroundings if you need to.

02

Repeat at a useful moment. Attach the pause to something you already do. Notice whether that makes it easier to remember.

03

Notice an automatic reach. Before opening an app, ask what you want from it. Then decide whether to continue.

04

Remove one interruption. Change a notification or autoplay setting. Keep the functions and contact you need.

05

Try quiet in company. Spend a brief, comfortable pause beside someone you trust, with their agreement. Or repeat your own practice if you prefer.

06

Choose an ordinary gap. Try a minute without adding content while waiting somewhere safe. Notice the place you’re in.

07

Look back. What helped? What irritated you? What would you repeat? Decide on one small practice for next week.

After a pause, you can jot down a sentence or simply remember one observation. Writing by hand is optional. So is recording anything at all.

If you miss a day, continue when you remember. There’s no backlog of silence to complete on Sunday.

Optional stretch

If short pauses are useful, try a ten-minute period without entertainment feeds while you draw, make something or sit outside. Keep necessary technology available. You’re exploring what less input feels like, not entering a digital survival programme.

At the end of the week, “five minutes is too much, but a minute after lunch helps” is a useful result. So is “I prefer a quiet walk”. The aim is a practice you can use, not a number you can boast about.

Essential one07 / Beyond the timer

Take the pause
back into your life.

Practising quiet has a purpose beyond becoming good at sitting quietly. It gives you somewhere to rehearse not responding to every thought, feeling or interruption straight away.

You can try the same pause in ordinary moments.

Before sending the message

You’re annoyed and you’ve written a reply with impressive destructive potential. Leave it unsent for a moment. Read it again. What do you want to happen after the other person sees it?

You might still need to say something firm. A pause gives you a chance to choose words that say what you mean without creating a second problem.

When someone is talking

A friend tells you something difficult. You don’t have to fill the first gap with advice or a story about when something similar happened to you.

Give them time. You could ask, “Do you want me to listen, or help you think about what to do?” Being present can involve words as well as quiet. It doesn’t mean keeping serious worries secret or trying to handle a crisis alone.

When a decision feels crowded

Subject choices, friendships, study, work and relationships can attract plenty of opinions. Before collecting more, take a moment to name what matters to you and what information you’re missing.

Your first feeling isn’t necessarily the right answer. Quiet doesn’t replace evidence or good advice. It can help you arrive at a conversation with a clearer question.

When you need a break

Not every pause needs to produce an insight. You can look out of a window without converting the experience into personal development. Sometimes you’ll notice a bird. Sometimes you’ll notice that the window needs cleaning. Both are allowed.

People use quiet in different ways, including reflection, meditation and religious practice. You can bring your own beliefs to it, or none. This chapter asks for no particular worldview and promises no special revelation.

Make enough room
to choose your next move.

That might mean putting the phone down. It might mean using it to call someone, look something up or ask for help. What matters is that you’ve noticed what you need and made a choice.

Essential one08 / Make it yours

A few questions.
One next step.

Choose a question to think about, write about or discuss. In a group, passing is always an option. Nobody owes the room a personal disclosure.

  1. When does your phone help you feel connected? When does it leave you feeling further away?
  2. What did you notice in a quiet minute that you hadn’t noticed beforehand?
  3. What makes a pause difficult for you: your surroundings, your responsibilities, your thoughts or something else?
  4. Which feels more useful right now: less input, more support from someone, or both?
  5. Where could a small pause help you make a choice this week?

My moment: After I…

My practice: I’ll pause for… and notice…

My adjustment: If that doesn’t suit me, I’ll try…

Before you move on

Use these as reflection prompts, not entry requirements for the next essential. On this page, ticks are temporary and are not saved.

Essential one, in short

A quiet pause gives you a chance to notice what’s happening before adding more input. Start small, adapt it to your life and don’t force yourself through distress. The useful skill is noticing and choosing, not sitting perfectly still.

You can stop reading now. If it’s a suitable moment, look away from this page and notice something in the room. Stay there for a little while. Then decide what you want to do next.

For Brendan / Editorial review

Keep the idea.
Earn the reader’s trust.

The source’s best idea is practical: leave enough quiet in your day to notice what you think and feel. The late-night opening, small daily challenge and link to being present with other people are worth keeping. The main weakness is that the chapter repeatedly argues its case instead of letting the reader try it.

What changed, and why
  1. Replaced accusation with observation. “You have lost the ability to sit in silence” and claims about an entire generation (PDF pp. 15–18) assume too much about the reader. The revision asks them to investigate their own habits and acknowledges valuable online relationships.
  2. Removed unsupported precision and simplified neuroscience. The “96” phone checks and “2.5 hrs” focus tiles (p. 17) lack a usable source in the chapter. “Silence means no dopamine” is misleading. The rewrite drops the figures and explains the practical effects of autoplay, notifications and endless feeds without diagnosing addiction.
  3. Changed the safety advice. “Your brain… will panic”, “This is withdrawal” and “Push through it” (p. 19) should not be default instructions for a mixed-age audience. The new chapter distinguishes ordinary restlessness from distress, offers adaptations and clearly allows stopping and seeking support. It promises no timed progression into clarity.
  4. Made the Universal edition fully secular. Scripture-led wisdom boxes and spiritual encounter language (pp. 16, 19 and 31) become open reflection prompts. Readers can bring their own beliefs, but the chapter requires none.
  5. Removed unverified testimony. The “Real Story” accounts (pp. 24 and 27) have no verifiable provenance supplied here. Rather than presenting them as evidence, this version uses one explicitly imagined opening scene. This does not establish that the original accounts are false; publication as testimony would require provenance and permission.
  6. Rebuilt the practice around choice. The rigid five-to-twenty-minute ladder and repeated challenges (pp. 22–23 and 30) become a short introductory practice and an adaptable seven-day experiment. There is no compulsory streak or time target.
  7. Protected necessary technology use. Blanket bans on non-human alerts and phone-free solo walks (pp. 22, 26 and 30) overlook medical alerts, accessibility, caring responsibilities and safety. The new instructions keep those needs intact.
  8. Tightened the prose and widened the age fit. School, study, work and shared-home situations replace a university-heavy default. Light humour targets familiar habits rather than the reader. British spelling and punctuation are used throughout, with no em dashes.

Editorial trade-off: this is a substantial rewrite, not a light copy-edit. It gives up some of the source’s prophetic urgency to gain credibility, warmth and a practice readers can adapt. It keeps the core argument that attention and self-knowledge deserve space.

Final voice pass: removed repeated motivational endings, certainty about how a reader must feel, and decorative quotations. The final chapter above is the clean reading version.
