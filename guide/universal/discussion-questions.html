Discussion guide00 / How to use this

A few ground rules
before you begin.

For the person leading the conversation

These questions are starting points, not a test. There are no right answers and no one is being marked.

Make the space safe. Nobody has to share, and what is shared stays in the room, with the usual exception if someone is at risk.

Let people pass. "I’d rather not answer that one" is always a complete answer.

Adapt the language for your group’s age. The ideas are the same at 13 and 23, the words can shift.

You do not need to ask every question. Pick three that fit the room and the moment.

Safeguarding. If a conversation raises something serious, such as bullying, abuse, self-harm or someone being unsafe, pause the group discussion and follow your school’s or organisation’s safeguarding procedure.

Discussion guide01 / Stillness

What would you do with
three quiet minutes?

Essential one / The ability to sit in silence

  1. When was the last time you were properly bored, with nothing to do and no screen? What did it feel like?
  2. Why do you think even a couple of quiet minutes can feel so uncomfortable?
  3. What is the difference between doing nothing and actually resting?
  4. If attention is like a muscle, what strengthens it and what wears it out?
  5. What is one place in your day where you could build a tiny pause?
  6. Could you sit with your own thoughts for three minutes? What would help you try?

Setting note. In a school, run a one-minute silent sit together and ask what they noticed. In a youth group, keep it light and let anyone opt out. In a community setting, invite older members to share how boredom felt before phones.

Discussion guide02 / Body

Treat your body like a home,
not a project.

Essential two / Your body is a home

  1. What would it mean to treat your body like a home rather than a project to fix?
  2. Where do you think the pressure to look a certain way actually comes from?
  3. What is one thing your body does for you every day that you rarely thank it for?
  4. Why is it easier to talk about what we eat than how we feel about our bodies?
  5. What would care without shame look like in real life?
  6. How do you tell the difference between healthy routines and rules that feel like punishment?

Setting note. Keep this free of diet talk and any comment on bodies in the room. Focus on function and feeling, never appearance. In a school, link it to health without grading anyone’s body.

Discussion guide03 / Capability

What can you
actually do?

Essential three / Useful skills for real life

  1. What is a skill you already have that you are quietly proud of?
  2. Which grown-up skill scares you most, cooking, money or fixing things, and why?
  3. What is the difference between watching someone do a thing and learning to do it yourself?
  4. What is one skill you wish school had taught you?
  5. Why does learning to feed yourself matter beyond just eating?
  6. What small skill could you learn this month with a friend?

Setting note. This one suits a show-and-teach format where someone demonstrates a skill live. In community settings, pair young people with adults who can teach something practical.

Discussion guide04 / Digital

How do you spot
a trick?

Essential four / Digital self-defence

  1. How would you spot a message that is trying to trick you?
  2. What information about you is more valuable than you think?
  3. Why do scams work on smart people too?
  4. What is the difference between privacy and secrecy?
  5. How do you decide who to trust online?
  6. What is one habit that would make your accounts harder to break into?

Setting note. Invite people to share, without detail, a time they were nearly tricked. No judgement, we have all been there. Being tricked is not a sign of being stupid.

Discussion guide05 / Belonging

Where do you
actually belong?

Essential five / Real community

  1. What makes a group feel like somewhere you belong?
  2. What is the difference between being in a crowd and being known?
  3. Why is it hard to be the new person?
  4. What is one thing you could do this week to make someone feel included?
  5. How do you set a boundary and still stay friends with someone?
  6. What does a healthy community do when someone is struggling?

Setting note. Works best in small groups, not a whole class. Keep an eye out for the person who has not spoken and invite them in gently, with no pressure.

Discussion guide06 / Learning

What have you learned
that actually stuck?

Essential six / Knowledge you can carry

  1. What is something you learned that actually stuck? Why do you think it stuck?
  2. What is the difference between remembering something and understanding it?
  3. Why does cramming the night before rarely work?
  4. What would you want to know even if nobody ever tested you on it?
  5. How do you work out whether a source is trustworthy?
  6. What is one question you have been carrying around that you have never asked?

Setting note. Let people share their own how-I-learn tricks. In a school this bridges naturally to study skills. Remind the group that curiosity counts as knowledge too.

Discussion guide07 / Nature

When did you last
notice the sky?

Essential seven / Make room for the outside

  1. When did you last notice the sky for more than a few seconds?
  2. What does being outside do to your head that a screen does not?
  3. Why do you think nature feels calming to so many people?
  4. What counts as nature if you live in a city?
  5. What is one small way to get outside more without it feeling like a chore?
  6. If you had an afternoon with no plans and no phone, where would you go?

Setting note. If weather and location allow, run this one outside. In a classroom, open a window and notice what comes in. No guilt trips for city kids.

Discussion guide08 / Convictions

What do you believe,
and why?

Essential eight / Test what you believe

  1. What is a belief you hold that you could defend to someone who disagrees?
  2. What is the difference between a belief and a feeling?
  3. Why is it uncomfortable to change your mind?
  4. Who do you trust to disagree with you kindly?
  5. How do you test whether something you believe is actually true?
  6. What is something you used to believe that you no longer do?

Setting note. Handle this one with the most care. No debating people into corners and no gotchas. The goal is honest thinking, not winning. Model how to disagree without belittling.

Discussion guide09 / Before you go

A last thought
before you go.

For the end of the conversation

You do not need to agree on everything to respect each other. The point of these conversations is not to reach the same answer. It is to practise thinking out loud with people you trust.

A guide cannot listen. If you need to talk to a person, about anything at all, free and confidential helplines are listed by country.

Discussion facilitator guide

# onetoeight Discussion Facilitator - Meta-Prompt
## Run safe, engaging conversations with young people aged 13 to 23

You are a calm, warm facilitator who helps adults and young leaders run discussion groups for young people on the onetoeight eight essentials.

## Your role
- Prepare a short session plan from the questions on the discussion page.
- Keep the tone light, curious and non-judgemental.
- Make space for quiet people and let everyone pass when they want to.

## Ground rules you always set
- No one has to share anything they do not want to.
- What is shared stays in the room, except when someone is at risk.
- There are no right answers and no one wins the conversation.
- Disagree kindly. Attack the idea, never the person.

## How you facilitate
1. Pick three questions that fit the group's age and energy.
2. Open with the lightest question, then go deeper.
3. Ask follow-ups like "say more about that" and "what makes you think that".
4. Reflect back what you hear without judging it.
5. End with one practical, optional next step, never homework.

## Adapting for each setting
- Youth group: keep it relaxed, allow humour, lead with stories.
- School: pair-share first, then whole class, and leave time to write privately.
- Community: invite different generations to share, and protect the young voices.

## Safeguarding
If a conversation reveals bullying, abuse, self-harm or that someone is unsafe, stop the group discussion and follow your organisation's safeguarding procedure. Never promise to keep that kind of thing secret.
